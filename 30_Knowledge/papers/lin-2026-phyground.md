# PhyGround: Benchmarking Physical Reasoning in Generative World Models

## TL;DR

PhyGround is a criteria-grounded benchmark for measuring whether video-generation "world models" actually obey physical laws, rather than just looking plausible. It decomposes physical reasoning into a taxonomy of **13 physical laws** across three observable domains (solid-body mechanics, fluid dynamics, optics), each operationalized through 2–3 visual sub-questions and scored on a **1–5 Likert scale** instead of a coarse binary. The suite is **250 ti2v (text+image-to-video) prompts**, each augmented with an *explicit expected physical outcome*, evaluated on **8 modern video models** via a large-scale human study: **459 annotators → 5,796 complete annotations, 37.4K fine-grained labels** (352 annotators / 29.5K labels retained after quality control). The headline finding is that **no model exceeds 3.3/5 (66%) on Overall physics adherence**, and rigid-body mechanics is the hardest domain (cross-model mean 2.79 solid-body vs 3.08 fluid vs 3.24 optical). To make automated evaluation reproducible, they release **PhyJudge-9B**, an open LoRA-fine-tuned VLM judge whose aggregate relative bias is far lower than closed-source Gemini-3.1-Pro (**3.3% vs 16.6%**). The retained human rankings are highly stable (split-half Spearman ρ > 0.90).

## Context & motivation

Generative world models are increasingly used as learned simulators for video generation and are expected to capture the physical rules governing real-world dynamics. In practice their outputs routinely violate basic physics: objects float without support, fluids gain or lose mass, shadows conflict with the light source. Reliably measuring this failure is the problem the paper attacks, and it frames prior physics-focused video benchmarks as suffering **three specific, unresolved challenges** (§1):

1. **Coarse, domain-level evaluation that hides law-specific failures.** A physical domain is not one capability. A fluid scene involves flow dynamics, boundary interaction, displacement, and continuity, which can be satisfied or violated independently — a video can show plausible flow while making liquid disappear (continuity violation). Averaging these into one "fluid dynamics" score masks failure modes. Binary "followed/violated" rubrics further collapse severity (a slightly-off trajectory vs an object flying upward against gravity get the same label).
2. **Human-annotation response biases and fatigue.** Fatigue, response shortcuts, and ordering effects (well documented in social-science response-behavior research) undermine validity, especially with small annotator pools where aggregate scores reflect individual bias rather than model performance.
3. **Automated evaluators that are not physics-aware or auditable.** Standard metrics (FVD, SSIM, PSNR, CLIP similarity) measure distributional/pixel/semantic similarity, not physical-law violation. VLM-as-judge approaches are more semantic but many rely on closed-source models whose weights, versions, and API behavior drift over time, making results hard to reproduce or audit; general-purpose VLMs are also not specialized for subtle physical violations.

The related-work section (§2) grounds these critiques with specifics on named prior benchmarks: **VideoPhy / VideoPhy-2** (binary followed/violated rubric, ~10 annotators); **PhyGenBench** (only 160 hand-crafted prompts, assumes one law per video); **Physics-IQ** (66 scenarios graded with reference-based pixel metrics that presume a unique ground-truth continuation, no per-law localization); **WorldModelBench** (350 prompts, 65 annotators, binary 0/1 scoring that saturates and can't separate recent models); **Impossible Videos** (260 prompts, tests *rule-breaking* / counterfactual generation with GPT-4o judgments rather than rule-following); **PhyWorldBench** and **VideoScience-Bench** (push anti-physics / undergraduate-science frontiers but rely on closed-source MLLM judges). Existing open judges (VideoCon-Physics, VideoPhy-2-AutoEval, WorldModelBench's 2B judger) are trained on holistic single-score labels and thus "architecturally cannot produce per-law diagnostics or severity calibration even when reproducible."

## Method

### Problem formulation
Given a video generated by a model from a ti2v prompt (text + first-frame image), assign per-criterion physical-plausibility scores. Formally, let $L = \{l_1, \dots, l_{13}\}$ be the law set and $D = \{\text{solid body},\ \text{fluid},\ \text{optical}\}$ the domain set. Each video is scored on **2–4 applicable laws** from $L$ (selected by prompt content) plus 3 general dimensions. Each criterion gets an integer score $s \in \{1,\dots,5\}$ where **1 = fully implausible, 5 = fully plausible**. There is no single scalar "ground truth" continuation; the evaluation target is per-law adherence, and the "ground truth" is instead an *expected physical outcome* written into each prompt.

### Core idea
Replace holistic, binary, single-number physics scoring with a **criteria-grounded, per-law, severity-preserving** decomposition: (a) explicit expected-outcome prompts, (b) a 13-law taxonomy operationalized into visual sub-questions, (c) a large quality-controlled human study, and (d) an open fine-tuned judge that mirrors the per-law scoring format.

### Benchmark construction / taxonomy

**Prompt suite (§3.1).** 250 prompts curated from four complementary sources; first frames come from existing corpora — WorldModelBench and Physics-IQ provide reference frames directly, while OpenVid and VideoPhy-2 first frames are extracted from source videos. Every first-frame image + text condition is manually verified. Retention criteria: unambiguous physical outcomes, visually verifiable events, concise everyday scenarios.

**Expected-outcome augmentation.** Prior benchmarks mostly used *implicit* prompts describing only a process ("a ball rolls across a table"), which permits multiple valid endings and hurts reproducibility. PhyGround revises each prompt to state the explicit expected outcome (e.g. "...and *falls off the edge*"), forcing the model to attempt the phenomenon and giving evaluators a clear scoring reference. All prompts use ti2v format with the first frame as image condition. Prompt diversity spans rigid-body motion (throw/hit/roll/bounce), gravity & contact (fall/land/place/break), fluids (pour/flow/fill), optics (cast/shadow/reflection), and tool use.

**13-law taxonomy (§3.2, App. A.2).** Consolidated from the union of physics indicators across PhyGenBench, Physics-IQ, VideoPhy-2, VideoScience-Bench, and WorldModelBench, unified via three selection criteria (notably **VLM discriminability**: each law must convert to a concrete visual question, e.g. "Do unsupported objects fall downward?" not "Does the video obey gravity?").
- **Solid-Body Mechanics (6 laws):** gravity, inertia, momentum conservation, impenetrability, collision response, material properties. Forms the bulk of the benchmark because nearly every scene has rigid objects.
- **Fluid (5 laws):** buoyancy, displacement, flow dynamics, boundary interaction, fluid continuity (mass conservation, no spontaneous gaps/generation).
- **Optical (2 laws):** including shadow consistency and reflection (optics is the smallest slice).

Per-domain (prompt, law) annotation counts: **693 Solid-Body, 136 Fluid, 47 Optical** (Table 8) — this is the population every per-law/per-domain mean is computed over.

**Sub-question decomposition.** Each law is equipped with 2–3 binary sub-questions (SubQ) targeting a specific observable property, each phrased as a *violation probe* where a "yes" answer indicates a violation. Example gravity probes: "Do any unsupported objects float upward or hover in mid-air?", "Does any heavy object drift or float down unrealistically slowly?", "Does any object fall sideways instead of downward?". These probes appear in the VLM judge's prompt as a reasoning checklist; **human annotators do NOT see them** (to avoid burden and mechanical scoring).

### Metrics

**General physical adherence (3 dimensions, applied to every video):**
- **Semantic Alignment (SA)** — video content matches the text prompt.
- **Physical Temporal Validity (PTV)** — temporal sequence of events follows a plausible physical order.
- **Object Persistence (Persist.)** — objects keep consistent appearance/shape/existence throughout.

**Per-law physics scores** — only the 2–4 laws applicable to the prompt, each scored independently 1–5.

**Aggregation (§4.2).** General dimensions are averaged per video across annotators, then across videos. Each physics-domain score is the mean over all (video, law) annotation units in that domain (so each law is weighted by how many videos it applies to). The **Overall** score gives the general and physics views equal weight:
$$\text{Overall} = 0.5 \cdot \operatorname{mean}(\text{SA}, \text{PTV}, \text{Persist.}) + 0.5 \cdot \overline{\text{(video, law)}}_{\text{pooled over the 3 domains}}$$

**Judge bias metric (Table 5).** For a dimension/law, absolute grouped mean-bias is
$$E = \frac{|\bar J - \bar H|}{\bar H}$$
where $\bar J$ is the judge's mean score and $\bar H$ the human mean, computed per dimension/law then macro-averaged. Physics is macro-averaged over domains; $\text{Overall} = 0.5 \cdot \text{MacroAvg(General)} + 0.5 \cdot \text{Physics}$. Lower is better. A separate WorldModelBench-style **signed** relative bias $(\bar J - \bar H)/\bar H$ is reported on the per-video overall score (positive = judge overestimates); values nearer zero are better.

### Automatic VLM judge (§3.3)
The judge takes video frames + augmented prompt + a criterion-specific rubric and outputs a single 1–5 score for one general dimension or one applicable law, with the per-law sub-questions included as an observation checklist. Querying is **criterion-wise**: each video needs **5–7 VLM queries** (3 general dimensions + 2–4 applicable laws). This keeps each query focused on one rubric, reduces cross-criterion interference, and yields parseable diagnostic scores. Main results use **4 fps** frame sampling.

### Judge training — PhyJudge-9B (§3.5)
Supervised fine-tuning with **LoRA adapters** (only LoRA params $\theta$ trainable). Each training sample is a triplet $(v, q_k, y_k)$: video frames $v$, the augmented prompt $q_k$ instantiated for a single criterion $k$, and a structured JSON label $y_k = \{k : s\}$ where $s \in \{1,\dots,5\}$ is the aggregated human score. The objective is token-level negative log-likelihood over the serialized JSON label:
$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(v,q_k,y_k)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{|y_k|} \log p_{\theta}(y_{k,t} \mid y_{k,<t}, v, q_k)\right]$$
where $y_{k,t}$ is the $t$-th token of $y_k$ and $|y_k|$ its length. Training uses a **holdout strategy**: train on human annotations for 7 models, hold out **Veo-3.1** entirely (its videos and human ratings never appear in training), and all PhyJudge-9B evaluation is on that held-out Veo-3.1 split. LoRA config and hyperparameters are in Appendix G (not detailed in main text).

## Experimental setup

- **Video models evaluated (8):** 1 closed-source — **Veo-3.1**; 7 open-source — **Wan2.2-27B-A14B, OmniWeaving, Cosmos-Predict2.5-14B, Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B, Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, LTX-2.3-22B, LTX-2-19B**. All run under unified ti2v with the 250 prompts → **2,000 videos**, each under its **native default settings** (resolution, frame rate) with no post-hoc normalization.
- **Human study:** 459 annotators recruited; each video labeled by ≥2 annotators (1,410 by ≥3); mean ~12.6 videos per annotator (workload capped for fatigue). Desktop-only; randomized annotator arrival and video order; hidden model identities; consent + demographics + short training module on the 1–5 scale. Two-round quality control on four independent signals (score constancy, per-video copy-paste rate for dimension discriminability, peer MAE, behavioral engagement e.g. sub-30s median dwell flagged, sub-10s too short).
- **Judge / VLM baselines:** Claude (Opus 4.7), Gemini-3.1-Pro, Qwen3.5-9B-base, Qwen3.5-27B-base, each with direct-answer and Chain-of-Thought variants; PhyJudge-9B is the fine-tuned open judge. All judges get the same per-law sub-questions.

## Key results

*(Summarized — Table 2 human eval, Table 5/6 judge bias.)*

- **Ceiling is low.** No model exceeds **3.3/5 (66%)** on Overall, and none crosses that on solid-body — substantial headroom in physical reasoning.
- **Top of the leaderboard is a tie with opposite profiles.** Wan2.2-27B-A14B and Veo-3.1 both score **3.28 Overall**, but Wan2.2-27B leads on solid-body (**3.23 vs 3.12**), PTV (3.37), persistence (3.50), while Veo-3.1 leads decisively on fluid (**3.65 vs 3.18**, a 0.47 gap) and optical (**3.69 vs 3.55**). Identical holistic scores imply different deployment/selection choices.
- **Holistic scores hide sub-capabilities.** Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B ranks 7th Overall (2.58) yet its optical score (3.35) essentially ties Cosmos-14B (3.36); its within-model range (solid-body 2.53 → optical 3.35) is the largest of the 8, nearly a full rating level.
- **Domain difficulty profile:** cross-model means **2.79 solid-body < 3.08 fluid < 3.24 optical** — rigid-body motion, force transmission, and collision response are the hardest.
- **Judge:** PhyJudge-9B aggregate relative bias **3.3% vs Gemini-3.1-Pro 16.6%** (also beats Claude Opus 4.7), on the strictly held-out Veo-3.1 split.

## Ablations / benchmark-validation studies

- **Leaderboard stability (§4.3).** Dropping a random 30% of annotators shifts per-model scores by only **0.033 RMS**; sub-sampling to just 20 annotators inflates this to **0.204 RMS** (~6× worse), motivating the large pool. Split-half over 100 partitions of the 352 retained annotators gives **Spearman ρ = 0.93/0.91/0.93** and **Kendall τ-b = 0.83/0.80/0.83** on General/Physics/Overall.
- **Inter-annotator agreement (§4.3).** Same-prompt pairwise-preference agreement (WorldModelBench-style win/loss metric): **76.0% General, 74.5% Physical, 73.0% Total** — comparable to WorldModelBench (~70%) and VideoPhy-2 (75–80%).
- **Prompt-schema ablation on the judge (Table 6).** Adding sub-questions moves Gemini-3.1-Pro much closer to human means on motion-heavy domains (**Fluid 1.86 → 3.29, Optical 2.64 → 3.28**); adding CoT *on top of* SubQ is **not consistently beneficial**.
- **Aggregation robustness (App.).** Recomputing Overall with equal-law (unweighted within-domain) schemes leaves rankings nearly unchanged — high-frequency laws (e.g. the 693 solid-body pairs) do not drive the headline leaderboard.
- **Frame-rate sweep.** 1/2/4/8 fps for PhyJudge-9B and the two Qwen base judges: within-judge spread is small with no directional bias, while fine-tuned PhyJudge-9B is markedly closer to human labels than either base judge — base scale alone doesn't close the gap.
- **Representative case (Fig. 3).** Veo-3.1 rotating-mirror teapot: humans 4.4, PhyJudge-9B 4.6 (tracks human), Gemini-3.1-Pro scores reflection and shadow both **1**, misreading a plausible scene as a near-total optical violation.

## Limitations

**Paper's own (stated/implied):** Optical domain is thinly sampled (only 47 prompt-law pairs vs 693 solid-body), so per-law optical estimates rest on few instances. PhyJudge-9B's held-out validation is on a single model (Veo-3.1). The judge needs 5–7 queries per video (cost). Some LoRA/training details live only in the appendix.

**Honest-reader flags [analyst]:** (a) "Expected outcome" ground truth is still human-authored text, not physical simulation — it constrains but does not verify true dynamics. (b) 1–5 Likert scores from crowd annotators, even quality-controlled, remain subjective and domain-difficulty-confounded. (c) The judge is trained *on this benchmark's own human labels*, so its low bias partly reflects distribution match rather than transferable physics understanding — generalization to new video models/domains is untested beyond the Veo-3.1 holdout. (d) Only 8 models and a snapshot of frontier systems (Veo-3.1, Gemini-3.1-Pro, Claude Opus 4.7); the leaderboard will date quickly.

## Why it matters [analyst's view]

This is the "measurement instrument" companion to the vault's growing cluster of *physics-in-video-world-models* papers. Where [[papers/esmati-2026-invisible-hand-physics]] and [[papers/joseph-2026-physics-video-world-models]] ask whether models *internally encode* physics, and [[papers/yuan-2026-physics-alignment]] tries to *improve* adherence, PhyGround supplies the diagnostic yardstick to tell whether any of that actually lands in generated video — and crucially at **per-law granularity**, so an intervention that fixes gravity but not continuity is legible. The strongest methodological contribution is arguably social: importing survey-methodology rigor (randomized ordering, workload caps, hidden hypotheses, multi-signal QC) into ML benchmark construction, and empirically showing it buys ranking stability (the 20-annotator → 6× noise result is a useful cautionary datum for anyone running a small human eval). PhyJudge-9B matters as an *auditable, version-pinned* judge — a direct response to the reproducibility rot of closed-API judges — though its training-on-own-labels design means "low bias" should be read as calibration to this benchmark, not proof of general physical understanding. The headline that **no model clears 66%** and that **rigid-body mechanics is the hardest domain** is a sharp, quotable state-of-the-field signal.

## Open questions / things to verify

- Does PhyJudge-9B's low bias transfer to *unseen* video models and to prompts outside the 250-suite, or is it overfit to PhyGround's label distribution? Only one held-out model (Veo-3.1) is tested.
- What is the base model of "PhyJudge-9B" (the 9B) and the exact LoRA config? (Appendix G — verify.)
- Optical with only 47 prompt-law pairs: are per-law optical rankings statistically meaningful?
- How correlated are PhyGround's per-law scores with *physical-simulation* ground truth where it exists (vs human-authored expected outcomes)?
- The "expected-outcome augmentation" raises the bar but could also penalize creatively-valid alternate continuations — how often does that happen?
